# Practical Guide: Syncretic Event Documentation Methodology

## The "Depth Inquiry" Approach: Combining Insights from Multiple Alternative Perspectives

This methodology synthesizes insights from several alternative approaches to create a practical system for event documentation that captures phenomena with more nuance than traditional methods allow.

## The Four Pillars

### 1. **Phenomenological Witness Layer**
- Before any factual questioning, ask participants: "What was happening *in you* during this event?"
- Document immediate first-person descriptions of bodily sensations, emotional responses, and lived time
- Include what was *absent* from experience (what didn't happen that was expected)
- Give participants this instruction: "Don't tell me what happened to you. Tell me your experience of what was happening."

### 2. **Relational Mapping**
For each reported experience element:
- Ask: "What made this experience possible?" tracing material, social, and conceptual dependencies
- Create three concentric circles:
  - Circle 1: Immediate human participants
  - Circle 2: Material and technological mediators
  - Circle 3: Historical, political, and conceptual frameworks
- Practice "agential doubt": Assume everything could be otherwise

### 3. **Multiple Timeline Construction**
Instead of chronological sequence, construct:
- **Ripple lines**: How the event echoes in different communities and contexts
- **Braid lines**: How the same moment branches into different possible interpretations
- **Underground lines**: Processes (like bureaucratic decisions or long-term conditions) that made the event possible
- **Speculative extensions**: How participants imagine this event continuing into potential futures

### 4. **Reflexive Documentation**
Every stage of documentation becomes part of the documented event:
- Record the process of documentation itself
- Note how the act of questioning changes memory
- Document power dynamics in who gets to interpret what happened
- Include "failed" or incomplete documentation attempts

## Practical Application Framework

### Phase 1: Initial Encounter
1. Establish "documentation contract" with participants - explain you cannot capture the event, only multiply perspectives on it
2. Begin with silence: Allow 2-3 minutes of quiet reflection before any questioning starts
3. Start phenomenologically, not factually

### Phase 2: Multi-Sited Inquiry
1. Interview participants in their original witnessing location when possible
2. Use different interviewing methods for different participant relationships to the event (perpetrator, victim, witness, official, etc.)
3. Allow participants to question each other's accounts without requiring resolution

### Phase 3: Digital/Physical Integration
- Document how participants use digital tools (phones, social media) to make sense of or share their immediate experience
- Create "parallel documentation" showing online and offline evolution of the same moment
- Note when digital resharing changes the meaning of what happened originally

### Phase 4: Recursive Analysis
1. Return interviews and analyses to participants for correction and elaboration
2. Document how participants respond to your documentation of their experience
3. Create "living document" that evolves as participants remember differently over time
4. Conclude not with final truth, but with "documentation fatigue" - when the event becomes something else entirely

## Sample Documentation Questions

### For Phenomenological Layer:
"Where in your body did you feel this event?"
"Was there a moment when time seemed to collapse or expand?"
"What did the air taste like? What did your hands want to do?"
"What did you see that nobody else mentioned seeing?"

### For Relational Understanding:
"Who or what made it possible for you to have your particular experience?"
"What technologies, words, or physical arrangements shaped what you could perceive?"
"What did you see others not seeing because of your position?

### For Temporal Complexity:
"When did this event actually end for you?"
"What from this moment keeps happening in new contexts?"
"What past did this event bring forward for you?"
"How might this event be preemptively shaping your imagined future?"

### For Reflexive Awareness:
"How does being interviewed about this change your memory of what happened?"
"What am I not asking that you feel is essential about this experience?"
"If you had to lie about this experience to preserve its most important truth, what would you lie about?"

## Error Documentation Protocol
When documentation fails or becomes contradictory:
- Document the breakdown itself as part of the event's enduring legacy
- Ask participants: "In what ways has this attempt to document the event already changed it?"
- Accept that some events resist documentation and view this resistance as part of their meaning

This syncretic approach acknowledges that events don't exist independently of their documentation, but rather that the act of documentation itself becomes part of the ongoing becoming of whatever the "event" is. The goal shifts from capturing the event to participating ethically in its continued unfolding across time, space, and meaning-making practices.
